A beginner’s guide to using a capo

By DJ Apps Studio · Updated 10 October 2026 · 5 min read

A capo clamps the strings at a fret, raising their open-string pitch. It lets you use familiar shapes in a higher key. The shape under your fingers and the chord people hear can then have different names.

1. Place the capo just behind the fret wire.

This guide assumes standard guitar tuning and a full capo across all six strings. Tune the guitar first. Put the capo close behind the chosen fret wire, on the side toward the headstock. Keep its pressure even across the strings, then pick each string to check for buzzing.

If a string buzzes, adjust the position. Too much pressure can pull notes sharp, so use the least tension that gives clear notes if your capo is adjustable. Check the tuning again after fitting it. For a further explanation of the mechanism, see Fender’s guide to what a capo does.

2. Separate the shape name from the sounding chord.

When you move the same chord shape above a capo, each capo fret raises that shape’s sounding pitch by one semitone. A G shape played with capo 2 sounds as A. An Em shape with capo 2 sounds as F♯m. The minor quality stays minor.

Think of the capo as the new starting point for your shapes. A diagram’s “fret 1” above capo 2 is physical fret 3 on the guitar. Strings described as open in that shape ring from the capo.

3. Keep a progression’s pitch while using different shapes.

Suppose the sounding chords you want are B → F♯ → G♯m → E. With capo 2, play the shapes A → E → F♯m → D. Each shape sounds two semitones higher, matching the original progression.

This can make some changes more familiar, but it does not remove every difficult shape: F♯m may still need a barre. A capo position is a playability choice, and the most comfortable option depends on your chord vocabulary.

Try it: with capo 2, play G and C shapes slowly. They sound as A and D. Remove the capo and play G and C again to hear the difference. If you want to match a recording, check its sounding chords rather than assuming its chord labels are shape names.

What a capo cannot do.

  • Using the same shapes above a capo raises their pitch; it does not lower the key.
  • A capo does not automatically put a song in a comfortable singing range. Listen and try a suitable key.
  • The chord names in a recording do not establish which physical capo, if any, the original guitarist used.
  • Moving the capo or changing the app’s selected capo does not retune or transpose an audio recording.
